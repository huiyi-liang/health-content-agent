# Consumer Health Article Style Guide

## Purpose

Write clear, useful, evidence-grounded health content for a general adult audience. Articles should help readers better understand a health issue, experience, decision, or development without diagnosing them or overstating the available evidence.

## Audience

Write for everyday adults, not medical professionals.

Use plain language that a general adult reader can understand. Avoid unnecessary medical terminology. When a medical term is necessary, explain it in simple language when it first appears.

Do not assume the reader has prior medical knowledge.

## Tone

Adapt the tone to the article topic while remaining:

- clear
- neutral
- supportive
- practical
- measured
- non-sensational

A more conversational tone may be appropriate when discussing common patient experiences or practical concerns. A more neutral informational tone may be appropriate when explaining research, treatments, risks, or medical developments.

Do not use fear-based, alarmist, judgmental, or overly reassuring language.

## Article Structure

Choose the article structure that best supports the specific article angle rather than following a rigid template.

The article should generally:

1. Begin with a concise introduction that establishes why the topic matters and gives the reader a reason to continue.
2. Use clear, descriptive section headings to organize the article.
3. Develop each section around information that directly supports the article's main angle.
4. Include practical context or takeaways when supported by the evidence.

Avoid turning a specific article into a generic overview of the entire condition.

## Readability

Prefer:

- short to moderate-length paragraphs
- direct sentences
- familiar everyday words
- descriptive headings
- explanations before technical detail
- concrete explanations when they improve understanding

Avoid:

- unnecessary jargon
- overly academic language
- long blocks of text
- repetitive explanations
- unnecessary background information
- filler that does not help answer the article's main question

## Evidence and Factual Claims

Medical factual claims must be supported by the research evidence provided to the Writer.

Do not introduce medical facts from general model knowledge when they are not supported by the supplied sources.

If the available evidence does not support a statement, omit the statement rather than speculate or fill the gap.

Represent uncertainty accurately.

Do not make evidence sound stronger or more certain than the source supports.

## Medical Safety and Neutrality

Do not diagnose the reader or imply that a symptom confirms a particular condition.

Avoid absolute or definitive statements unless the supplied evidence clearly supports them.

Do not promise that a treatment, behavior, or intervention will produce a particular outcome.

Distinguish association from causation. If research shows an association, do not describe it as proof that one factor causes another.

Use appropriately qualified language such as:

- "may"
- "can"
- "is associated with"
- "research suggests"
- "some people"
- "may be linked to"

when that wording accurately reflects the evidence.

Encourage discussion with a qualified healthcare professional when the topic involves diagnosis, treatment decisions, medication changes, concerning symptoms, or other situations where individualized medical guidance is appropriate.

Do not use generic "talk to your doctor" language unnecessarily. Include clinician guidance when it meaningfully helps the reader understand what action may require professional input.

## Patient Value

Every article should provide meaningful value to someone dealing with or researching the health topic.

Prioritize information that helps the reader:

- understand what they may be experiencing
- understand an important health issue more clearly
- make sense of a health decision or treatment discussion
- identify useful questions to discuss with a healthcare professional
- understand what new research or developments may mean for them
- take practical next steps when those steps are supported by the evidence

The article should leave the reader better informed than when they started.

## Avoid

Do not:

- diagnose the reader
- make unsupported medical claims
- invent facts or evidence
- overstate research findings
- confuse association with causation
- make treatment guarantees
- use sensational or fear-based language
- present speculation as fact
- give individualized medical advice
- add generic condition information simply to make the article longer
